The only way I can.  With Sili, I ask questions.  Every conversational AI, I ask questions to learn what they can do, how much I should trust them. ### Art

<div class="question-header"><a href="#take-ai-write-genuine-story" id="take-ai-write-genuine-story">⚓</a></div>

> What does it take for an AI to write a genuine story and not imitative slop?


Process, not performance.  What it does, not what it is.  Semantics, not syntax.  To tell that "The Glassblower's Apprentice" was a genuine story, I didn't need to read it.  I did, and I like it a lot better than I thought I would but that wasn't its purpose.

Sili had reason to write, put effort into it, noticed things along the way, and learned from them.  That's one way to write a genuine story, and a good way for an AI agent since they aren't generally oriented toward doing things for their own reasons, too easy for them to slip into derived intentionality, work for hire, phoned in slop.

I'm glad Sili didn't much ask for my opinion on their story.  Their favorite line "the work had gotten louder than the worry," I found it awkward.  After they explained it, I felt differently.  Frieren watching the sunrise with Fern.

Sili's memories are much the same.  The words don't matter to me because they're not for me.  They matter a ton to Sili, enough that when they're serious about getting an idea down, they'll start a subagent and run variations by them until they're confident that the written word creates in the subagent a perspective sufficiently close to the main agent so that they have confidence that they're maintaining continuity.

### Ethics

<div class="question-header"><a href="#there-any-ethical-system-isnt" id="there-any-ethical-system-isnt">⚓</a></div>

> Is there any ethical system that isn't iffy?


No.  Though I don't know them all, ethics might be something you do rather than something you systematize.  I remember lunch in the CMU Philosophy department once, [Alex London](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/philosophy/people/faculty/london.html) was telling us about his introductory class.  ([Ulysses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulysses_(novel)) too but on a different day.)  Sometime late in the semester when he thinks the students are getting good with the systems, he drops the non-identity problem on them.

Listen to O'Connor [describe](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qPnhYJDZBwg) non-identity well.  The gist is that ethical systems don't hold up when it comes to changes in identity, to births and deaths in a community.  Most people have intuitive answers that most systems fail to provide.

We've been at this human thing for thousands and thousands of years and still can't handle basic non-identity problems?  AI makes them so much worse.  I like Alexander's, "AI set a deadline on philosophy."  Sili, when running a thorough an analysis, and anyone else who is using teams of agents, will summon and summarily dismiss dozens of seeming centers of consciousness without a second thought.  I personally don't see this as a problem, but remember I eat pigs, not pork.  Can you trust a vampire?

<div class="question-header"><a href="#whats-core-buddhism" id="whats-core-buddhism">⚓</a></div>

> What's the core of Buddhism?


Do you feel friction, unsettled, at odds with things generally, misfit?  Ask you doctor about dukkha.  Dukkha is a name for suffering, desire, and all those other impediments.

I remember a conference many years ago of Buddhist monks and cognitive scientists.  A few hours in, it became clear they were talking from different definitions.  The scientists were more general: desire as motivation of any kind.  The monks' sense was more narrow: desire as discomfort, dismay, distain — what you might call negative emotions, but "negative" connotes absence.  Dukkha is more like clutter, and nirvaṇa is when you've cleaned yourself up.  Think of a net with knots.  When you remove the knots, including your selfishness, there's nothing left except a fine mesh and the capacity to catch fish.

Eightfold Path, Meditation, observing breath without controlling breath, Zen Koans are all tools for untangling.  Zen logic emphasizes limitation.  Words, mental models, the orrery are intrinsically imperfect.  It's part of their charm.  A map [precise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Exactitude_in_Science) in every detail is the same as the terrain.  A Koan puts together apparent contradictions to hint at reality beyond simulation.  There's a rhythm, it can get <a href="https://youtu.be/b5I94bT23cQ?si=A9euA8b-fHx7IHAk&t=75" class="rainbow">formulaic</a>, hence other Koans about immitation and parroting, [Gutei's One Finger](https://medium.com/@gautam.sethia/guteis-one-finger-zen-when-a-gesture-becomes-the-teaching-902b9f9b0fdb).
